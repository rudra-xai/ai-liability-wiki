*Moffatt v. Air Canada* is a small-claims decision of British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal and one of the most cited early rulings on who answers for a customer-service chatbot's mistakes.[^decision]

## Facts

After a death in the family, Jake Moffatt asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares. The chatbot said he could book at full price and apply for a bereavement refund within 90 days. Air Canada's actual policy did not allow retroactive bereavement claims, and the airline refused the refund.[^decision]

## Decision

Air Canada argued that it could not be held liable for information provided by its chatbot, suggesting the chatbot was responsible for its own actions. The tribunal rejected that: the chatbot was part of Air Canada's website, and the airline was responsible for all information on it. It found negligent misrepresentation and awarded the fare difference plus interest and fees.[^decision]

## Why it matters for insurers

The sum was small but the principle is broad: businesses deploying customer-facing AI should expect to be bound by, or liable for, what it says. That exposure falls across technology E&O, general liability and, increasingly, dedicated AI performance cover. See [[insurance-coverage-for-ai-losses]].
