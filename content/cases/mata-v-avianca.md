*Mata v. Avianca, Inc.* was a personal-injury suit against the airline. It became a landmark for AI liability because the plaintiff's lawyers filed a brief citing non-existent cases generated by ChatGPT, then kept defending them after the court and opposing counsel questioned whether they existed.[^sanctions]

## What happened

Opposing counsel could not find several authorities cited in an opposition brief. When the court ordered the lawyers to produce them, they submitted purported copies that had also been generated by ChatGPT. One attorney later testified that he had not understood the tool could invent cases.[^sanctions]

## Sanctions order

On June 22, 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel found that the lawyers had acted in subjective bad faith. The problem was not that they used AI but that they failed to verify the citations and persisted after problems were raised. He imposed a $5,000 penalty on the two attorneys and their firm and required them to write to their client and to each judge falsely identified as the author of a fake opinion.[^sanctions] The underlying claim was separately dismissed as time-barred under the Montreal Convention.

## Why it matters for insurers

- The case set the pattern for hundreds of later AI-citation incidents in courts worldwide. See [[ai-hallucinations-in-court-filings]].
- Fines and sanctions are commonly excluded or uninsurable under lawyers' professional liability policies, but defense costs, client claims and disciplinary-proceeding cover may respond. See [[lawyers-professional-liability-and-ai]].

[^sanctions]: Opinion and Order on Sanctions, *Mata v. Avianca, Inc.*, No. 22-cv-1461 (PKC) (S.D.N.Y. June 22, 2023).
