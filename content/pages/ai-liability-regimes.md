Policymakers debating AI liability have mostly chosen between three models.

## Fault-based liability

Claimants must prove the defendant failed to take reasonable care. Supporters say it encourages innovation and targets blameworthy conduct. Critics say AI's opacity makes proving fault and causation unrealistically hard. The withdrawn [[eu-ai-liability-directive]] would have eased this burden with disclosure orders and presumptions.

## Strict liability

Producers pay for damage caused by defects regardless of fault. The EU's revised [[eu-product-liability-directive]] extends this to software. Supporters say it places costs on those best able to insure and reduce risk; critics warn of over-deterrence and limited insurance capacity for correlated AI losses.

## Hybrids and alternatives

Proposals include risk-based tiers, safe harbors for compliance with standards (as in the [[colorado-ai-act]]), mandatory insurance for high-risk systems and compensation funds. Scholars such as [[ryan-abbott]] have argued for treating AI and human conduct under comparable standards.

## Current debate

At the October 2026 Dublin summit, industry leaders questioned whether regulation keeps pace with advanced AI, while MEP Brando Benifei called for harmonized EU civil-liability rules.
