AI-related losses rarely fall neatly within one insurance policy. Depending on who was harmed and how, a claim may touch several existing lines, each written before generative AI and each with its own triggers and exclusions. This page maps the main lines and the gaps between them.

## Three kinds of AI loss

1. **Third-party liability**: someone sues because an AI system caused them harm, such as a wrong answer, a biased decision, a privacy breach or infringing output.
2. **First-party loss**: the business itself suffers loss, such as an outage, a model failure that halts operations, or a data breach.
3. **Performance shortfall**: the AI simply does not perform as promised. Traditional policies rarely cover this, and it is the gap specialist AI performance products aim to fill. See [[affirmative-ai-insurance-market]].

## Policy lines that may respond

| Line | Typical AI scenario | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Professional liability / E&O | Advice or a service delivered with AI is wrong | "Professional services" definitions; sanctions and fines exclusions |
| Lawyers' professional liability | Hallucinated citations; missed deadlines from AI tools | See [[lawyers-professional-liability-and-ai]] |
| Technology E&O | An AI product fails a customer | Contractual-liability and performance-guarantee exclusions |
| Cyber | Agent-driven intrusion; data leaked via AI tools | War, infrastructure and wrongful-collection exclusions |
| General liability | Bodily injury or personal and advertising injury from AI output | New generative-AI exclusions; see [[ai-exclusions-and-policy-wording]] |
| Media / IP | Infringing or defamatory AI output | IP exclusions and training-data carve-outs |
| D&O | Securities claims over overstated AI capabilities ("AI washing") | Conduct exclusions; regulatory-fine sublimits |
| Employment practices | Biased AI hiring or monitoring tools | Discrimination coverage; wage-and-hour exclusions |

## Defense costs versus indemnity

Many AI disputes end at an early stage or turn on novel legal questions. For policyholders, **defense-cost coverage** and the insurer's duty to defend can matter as much as limits for damages. Courts construe ambiguous exclusions against insurers when deciding the duty to defend, as in [[citizens-insurance-v-wynndalco]].

## Silent AI

"Silent AI" describes AI exposure that a policy neither expressly covers nor excludes, much as "silent cyber" did a decade ago. Insurers are now resolving that ambiguity with affirmative grants, exclusions or both, so renewal wording deserves close review.
