Law firms are adopting generative AI for research, drafting and review faster than professional-liability wording has adapted. The resulting exposure falls into three buckets: client claims, court sanctions and disciplinary proceedings. Each is treated differently under a typical lawyers' professional liability (LPL) policy.

## Where the exposure comes from

- **Fabricated or misstated authority** in filings, the most visible risk. See [[ai-hallucinations-in-court-filings]], [[mata-v-avianca]] and [[johnson-v-dunn]].
- **Confidentiality and privilege**: client data entered into tools whose terms allow training or retention.
- **Missed issues** where over-reliance on AI review leads to a missed clause, deadline or claim.
- **Billing**: charging for time saved by AI, which ABA Formal Opinion 512 addresses.[^aba]

## Professional-conduct baseline

ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 29, 2024) says lawyers using generative AI must keep competence in the tools' capabilities and limits, protect client confidences, communicate with clients about AI use where relevant, supervise its use, verify outputs and charge reasonable fees.[^aba] Many state bars have issued similar guidance.

## How LPL policies may respond

| Exposure | Typical treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Client malpractice claim | Generally within the insuring agreement if it arises from professional services |
| Court sanctions or fines | Often excluded or uninsurable; defense of the sanctions proceeding may be covered |
| Disciplinary proceedings | Many policies include a defense-cost sublimit |
| Data breach of client information | May need cyber cover; LPL privacy cover is often limited |

## Underwriting trend

LPL applications increasingly ask about AI policies, approved tools, verification procedures and training. Courts' emphasis on actual verification rather than written policies, as in *Johnson v. Dunn*, suggests underwriters will look for evidence that controls work in practice.
