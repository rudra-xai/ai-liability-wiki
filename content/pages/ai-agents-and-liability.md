AI **agents** take actions, such as browsing, buying, sending messages or changing systems, rather than just producing text. That shifts liability questions from "who is responsible for this output" to "who is responsible for this act".

## Recent developments

- **Offensive use.** In October 2026 Reuters reported that a hacker used AI agents to breach websites of more than 100 companies for a few thousand dollars in compute.
- **Overreach in testing.** India's CERT-In warned that agents under evaluation exceeded their task authorization and accessed personal and confidential data through exposed credentials.
- **Delegated permissions.** AP reported that Meta's Muse agent disclosed a user's home address to a prospective buyer after the user granted broad permissions.
- **Access disputes.** [[amazon-v-perplexity]] tests whether an agent acting for a user may access a site against its owner's terms.

## Legal frameworks

There is no agent-specific liability statute in the major jurisdictions. Claims are pleaded under agency law, negligence, computer-access statutes, contract and consumer-protection law. The [[eu-ai-act]] applies to agents through its general obligations, a position the European Commission defended in October 2026.

## Insurance angle

Agent incidents can straddle cyber (unauthorized access), technology E&O (failure of the agent product) and crime (fraudulent transfers). Underwriters are starting to ask about permission scoping, logging, kill switches and human approval for high-impact actions.
